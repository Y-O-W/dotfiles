# Make event titles carry the team's time

Muhlert Digital should treat Google Calendar as **a structured diary that feeds Productive**, not as a to-do list. The single highest-leverage change is a **machine-readable title convention (`CLIENT SERVICE: activity`)**. Productive copies the event title into the time-entry note, and its AI reuses the service from past entries for recurring events, so consistent titles cut the manual "which budget, which service?" step that the team already finds hard ("Where do I book reporting?"). Around that convention, the evidence supports a small set of practices: schedule important work as concrete time blocks (implementation intentions, d = .65), size blocks from past actuals plus a buffer (the planning fallacy), cluster meetings and protect contiguous focus time (fragmentation and attention residue), log time the same day (diary beats recall), and end the workday with a shutdown that supports detachment. Several popular rules are conventions, not science, and should be adopted as team agreements rather than sold as research: 25/50-minute meetings, no-meeting days, colour legends, 90-minute work cycles and Pomodoro. Expect modest gains. Time management correlates only about r = .25 with job performance and more strongly with lower distress, so the honest pitch to the team is **more control and less stress, plus cleaner billing data**, not a productivity multiplier. German law raises the stakes: since the BAG's 2022 ruling, employers must record the start, end and duration of daily working time. Productive's project entries do not automatically satisfy that duty, and any works council co-determines how the tracking system is set up.

## The evidence backs concrete plans, buffers and fewer switches, not productivity magic

The best-quantified finding is modest. A 2021 meta-analysis of 158 studies (N = 53,957) found time management correlates **about r = .25 with work performance** and **r = −.36 with psychological distress**. The authors conclude it is "primarily a wellbeing enhancer, rather than a performance booster" ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245066)). Most of the included studies are cross-sectional, and conscientiousness correlates r = .45 with time management, so part of the effect is personality rather than practice. An earlier review of 32 studies reached the same conclusion: the clearest link is to **perceived control of time** and lower stress, while the link to performance was unclear ([Claessens et al. 2007](https://www.emeraldinsight.com/doi/abs/10.1108/00483480710726136)). For a team rollout, the realistic goal is that people feel in control of their week, and the cleaner Productive data is a by-product.

Three findings convert directly into calendar mechanics. First, **implementation intentions** ("at time X in place Y, I will do Z") raise goal attainment by **d = .65 across 94 tests** with over 8,000 participants ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)). A time block with a specific task in it is the calendar form of an implementation intention. That is an inference, because no controlled trial compares time blocking with to-do lists in knowledge workers. Second, the **planning fallacy** is large. In the classic study, students predicted they would finish their thesis in 33.9 days, actually took 55.5, and **missed even their own worst-case estimate** of 48.6 days ([Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)). Blocks should therefore be sized from past actuals, which Productive already holds as time entries, with explicit buffer time. No study fixes the right buffer size. Third, knowledge work is fragmented: field observation found people stay in one "working sphere" for only about 10–12 minutes and that **57% of spheres are interrupted** ([Mark et al. 2005](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Mark-CHI05-p321-mark.pdf); [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx)). Switching leaves "attention residue" that hurts the next task, and a **brief ready-to-resume note before switching measurably reduces it** ([Leroy & Glomb, via UW Bothell](https://www.uwb.edu/news/2019/02/12/task-interrupted)). That note is the one transition ritual with direct experimental support.

The meeting evidence points toward reducing and batching meetings, not banning them. Heavy meeting load hurts wellbeing most for achievement-oriented workers ([Rogelberg et al. 2006, APA](https://www.apa.org/monitor/jun06/frustrate)). Microsoft telemetry shows about half of meetings land in the 9–11 and 13–15 windows, people are interrupted roughly every two minutes, and **Tuesday carries 23% of the week's meeting load** ([Microsoft WorkLab 2025](https://microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/breaking-down-infinite-workday)). The often-quoted no-meeting-day study (76 companies) reported its largest self-reported productivity gains at about three meeting-free days. **Eliminating meetings entirely lowered satisfaction, engagement and cooperation**, and the design was non-randomised with self-reported outcomes ([Laker et al. 2022](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/102394/1/The%20Surprising%20Impact%20of%20Meeting-Free%20Days.pdf); [MIT SMR](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-surprising-impact-of-meeting-free-days/)).

Breaks and recovery follow the same pattern: real effects, but smaller than the lore claims. Micro-breaks of 10 minutes or less raise vigour (d = .36) and reduce fatigue (d = .35), but **do not reliably improve demanding cognitive work** (d = .16, not significant) ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)). Pomodoro has only small student studies with no outcome advantage, and one found faster fatigue ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/); [2025 follow-up](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)). Scheduling hard work by chronotype helped in only **45% of studies of adults aged 18–45** ([Chauhan et al. 2025](https://tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07420528.2025.2490495)). Psychological detachment after work is protective. Off-job work-related activity has a medium negative relationship with detachment ([Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)), and detachment can be trained (d = .36) ([meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34096763/)). This bears on the user's Sunday and evening planning sessions, discussed below. No study was found that measures the effect of shared or transparent team calendars on coordination, so every team-level calendar norm below is extrapolated from individual-level evidence and company case studies.

| Recommendation | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Put important work in specific time blocks | Implementation intentions meta-analysis (d = .65) | Strong, by analogy |
| Size blocks from past Productive actuals, add buffer | Planning fallacy | Strong for the bias; buffer size is a judgment call |
| Cluster meetings; protect contiguous focus time | Fragmentation field studies; attention residue experiments | Moderate (mechanism solid, intervention untested) |
| Write a ready-to-resume note when leaving a block | Leroy & Glomb experiment | Moderate |
| Log time the same day | Diary vs recall studies | Moderate |
| Fixed end-of-day shutdown, no work planning at home | Detachment meta-analyses | Moderate |
| Short breaks for fatigue, not output | Micro-break meta-analysis | Moderate (small k) |
| No-meeting day(s) | Laker et al., company case studies | Weak (self-reported) |
| 25/50-minute default meetings | GitLab and Google guidance | Convention, untested |
| Chronotype scheduling, Pomodoro, 90-minute cycles | Mixed or no evidence | Weak or lore |

## The four open questions on the Notion page have concrete answers

The Notion page "Software › Calendar" defines two calendars (personal and work), a Sunday weekly and an evening daily planning session, and four work event types along two axes: busy or free, and time-tracked or not ([Notion page](https://app.notion.com/p/yowfactor/Software-Calendar-421ed4002a8080348bc4f29386228a7b)). The same taxonomy appears in notes from a 20.11.26 meeting with Sebastian during the Productive rollout, so it is already a team artefact and not only a personal one ([Productive Setup page](https://app.notion.com/p/3abed4002a8080dd8865e3daff46ab46)). The page ends with four questions. Each one below gets an answer, with any uncertainty stated.

**Q1: Which calendar should be shared or connected to which?** The answer is **both sharing and copying, each for a different purpose**, and the copying can be automated.

*Sharing* the personal calendar with the Muhlert account (subscribed, ideally at "See only free/busy") does two things. First, you see both schedules side by side ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/6294878?hl=en)). Second, **appointment schedules can check it for conflicts**: they can check "your primary calendar and any calendars that you own, manage, or are subscribed to", and Busy events in a checked calendar hide the matching booking slots. This requires an eligible Workspace or Google One plan; Muhlert's Business Standard should qualify ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/16287054?hl=en)). Google does not say whether a calendar shared from another account at free/busy level counts, so test it with one booking page before relying on it.

*Sharing does not block coworkers' scheduling.* When a colleague uses "Find a time" or adds you as a guest, they see free/busy from your Muhlert primary calendar, not from calendars you subscribe to. Google's help pages are silent on which of your calendars are checked; the primary-only behaviour is stated by vendors, not Google ([Carly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/how-to-sync-work-and-personal-google-calendars/); [Reclaim](https://reclaim.ai/glossary/find-a-time)). Treat it as very likely but unconfirmed. For coworkers to see you as busy, a Busy block has to sit on the Muhlert calendar itself, so **copy** personal and YOWfactor events onto it as opaque "Busy" blocks, with title, attendees, description and location stripped. Never sync full details in the reverse direction, because work titles would then leak into the personal account.

Google has no native busy mirror across accounts, but copying need not be manual. Options, from least to most third-party access: a Google Apps Script on a timer that copies personal events as private Busy blocks (ready-made scripts exist, e.g. [google-calendar-sync](https://www.github.com/jbaruch/google-calendar-sync); the Muhlert admin must allow Apps Script and the script needs access to both calendars), a mirror add-on that updates and deletes placeholders when source events change ([Calendar Busy Sync](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/epkdohbenkblnenoeebclfbdbimfnnna), CalendarBridge, or a [Zapier template](https://zapier.com/templates/details/create-matching-busy-blocks-on-alternate-calendar-account)), or Claude reconciling the calendars during planning sessions ([vault note](file:///Users/yowfactor/Obsidian/Software/software_library/software_calendar.md)). The Claude route is only semi-automatic, because events added between sessions stay invisible until the next run. The recommended setup is free/busy sharing for booking pages plus an Apps Script one-way Busy mirror for coworkers. For visibility, display the Muhlert calendar inside the personal account read-only. Epic #40 already relies on this setup ([Issue #40](https://github.com/Y-O-W/dotfiles/issues/40)). Mirror only Busy events, and test recurring events, moves, deletes and declined invites, which are the known causes of "ghost" blocks. Working hours and Focus time do not transfer through a mirror.

**Q2: Are the many purpose calendars overkill?** Yes, for most of them. The connector currently lists 13 calendars, including separate business, business-event, personal, personal-event, health, event and routine calendars ([Issue #40](https://github.com/Y-O-W/dotfiles/issues/40)). Keep a calendar separate only when it differs in **who can see it, who owns it, or its default busy/free behaviour**. Category alone is not a reason, because 2026 Google Calendar handles categories better with colour: there are now 24 default colours plus up to **200 custom RGB colours per user** ([Workspace Updates, June 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/06/custom-event-colors-in-google-calendar.html)). Colours can be named as labels, and Time Insights then shows a time breakdown by label ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/12377581)). A defensible personal set is five or six calendars: Personal (private, events Busy), Health (private), YOWfactor business (Busy), Routine/Plan (default Free, so the generic weekly structure never blocks booking), the Muhlert work calendar, and optionally one Events calendar for options such as meetups and markets (Free). Health stays a separate calendar: its event descriptions hold sensitive notes, and a separate calendar lets the Busy mirror and AI agents treat it differently (free/busy only), which meets the "who can see it" test (see the calendar audit below). One caveat changes the cost of shared calendars: since 5 October 2026, **secondary calendars in Workspace are deleted with their owner's account**, so any team calendar (such as "Muhlert – Leave" or "Muhlert – Deadlines") should be owned by a durable account ([Workspace Updates, March 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/03/an-update-on-secondary-calendar-lifecycle-changes-and-a-new-API.html?m=1)).

**Q3: Can Claude connect to several Google calendars?** Within one account, yes, and it already does. The connector is signed in to the YOWfactor account and lists 13 calendars, including the Muhlert work calendar (`Muhlert Digital - Work`) and the personal Gmail calendar. An event listing on 24.11.26 returned the access role `reader` for the Muhlert calendar, so the connector can read it but **cannot write to it today** ([Issue #40](https://github.com/Y-O-W/dotfiles/issues/40); connector check, 24.11.26).

**One connection can write to both calendars if the Muhlert calendar is shared with edit rights.** The current direction, Muhlert shared into YOWfactor, is the right one. Each Workspace admin sets how far outside accounts may access its calendars, with four levels for primary calendars: free/busy only; all information, but outsiders cannot change calendars; all information, and outsiders can change calendars; all information, and outsiders can manage calendars ([Google Admin Help](https://support.google.com/a/answer/60765?hl=en)). Event details are already readable, so Muhlert allows at least the second level. For an agent to write, the Muhlert admin must allow the third or fourth, and the user then shares the Muhlert calendar with the YOWfactor account as "Make changes to events". Google's security guidance recommends limiting outside sharing to free/busy, so this is a request to the Muhlert admin, not a given. The reverse direction (YOWfactor shared into Muhlert, Claude connected to the Muhlert account) works technically but is not recommended: personal calendars would then be reached through the employer's account, the setup ends if the user leaves Muhlert, and it conflicts with Muhlert's plan for a restricted AI user with write approvals. A multi-account MCP server such as `nspady/google-calendar-mcp`, which the epic lists but has not tested, remains the fallback if Muhlert declines.

Writing through a shared calendar has limits. Focus time, Out of office and working location events exist "only on primary calendars", so they never work on secondary calendars ([Google Calendar API](https://developers.google.com/workspace/calendar/api/guides/calendar-status)). Google does not document whether someone with edit rights on another person's primary calendar can create them, so test it; if it fails, the agent writes a normal Busy block and the user converts it to Focus time in the Muhlert account. It is also unconfirmed which account invitations come from when an event with guests is created on a shared calendar, so the agent should write only blocks without guests on the Muhlert calendar, and the user sends meeting invites. Productive syncs from the Muhlert calendar, so agent-written blocks should reach it whichever account created them. Edit access also means Muhlert calendar data, including client names, is handled through the YOWfactor account and Claude, which Muhlert should approve under its GDPR and AI-governance rules. With edit access in place, the same connection (or the Apps Script from Q1) can write personal Busy blocks straight onto the Muhlert calendar, so Q1 needs no second connection. The steps are: ask the Muhlert admin for the "outsiders can change calendars" level, share the Muhlert calendar to the YOWfactor account with "Make changes to events", then test by creating and deleting one block and creating one Focus time event. A second route reaches Muhlert events indirectly: connecting Google Calendar to Productive also exposes it to Productive's AI Assistant, and the Productive MCP can read the resulting `calendar_events` ([Productive Help](https://help.productive.io/en/articles/6940914-integrating-your-google-calendar-with-productive)). Whatever the route, calendar text is a **prompt-injection channel**: an invite from outside can carry instructions. Keep the epic's rules in place: propose-then-apply, external text treated as data, the narrowest OAuth scope, never auto-accept invites, and log every write ([vault note](file:///Users/yowfactor/Obsidian/Software/software_library/software_calendar.md)).

**Q4: What naming convention lets AI agents derive time-tracking context?** Neither the scientific nor the practitioner literature has a standard. This is purely a design decision, and Productive's mechanics should drive it. When an event is converted, **the title pre-fills the time-entry note and the time range pre-fills the duration**. The user must still pick the budget and service manually ([Productive Help](https://help.productive.io/en/articles/6940914-integrating-your-google-calendar-with-productive)). Productive's AI conversion **reuses the service tracked last time for recurring events** and asks about one-off events ([Productive Help: AI conversion](https://help.productive.io/en/articles/14838945-converting-calendar-events-to-time-entries-with-ai)). A title that starts with a client code and a service code therefore gives both the human and any agent (Productive AI, or Claude through the Productive MCP) everything needed to choose the budget and service. Colour cannot do this job, because colours and labels are a **per-user display that only the owner and their editors can see** ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/12377581)). Only the title travels with the event to attendees and to Productive. The convention is specified in the next section.

## A one-page playbook: titles carry meaning, Busy means protected, colours stay personal

Everything in this section is a **convention**: a team agreement chosen for consistency and machine-readability, not a research finding. Where a rule rests on evidence, the evidence column says so. The aim is for Muhlert's calendar setup to run itself: each event's type follows from fields Google already has (guests, Show as, Focus time), and the title supplies the only information Google cannot, namely client, service and whether the time is tracked.

### Event types derive from Google fields, not from extra calendars

The Notion taxonomy maps directly onto native Google Calendar fields, so nobody has to tag types by hand. The one exception is "reminder", which should leave the event stream entirely.

| Type (Notion) | How it is encoded in Google | Show as | Time-tracked | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meeting | Event with guests | Busy | Yes | Agenda link required in the invite (GitLab practice) |
| Blocker | Focus time event for deep work, or a normal event for travel and appointments | Busy | Yes | Focus time auto-declines new invites |
| Flexible | Event without guests | **Free** | Yes | Admin, email, reporting; never blocks booking pages |
| Reminder | **Google Task**, or a Free event titled `NT:` | Free | No | Keeps untracked items out of Productive suggestions |
| Hold | Event titled `HOLD …` | Busy | No, until confirmed | Release or confirm within 48 h |
| Absence | Out of office (synced from Productive or Factorial) | Busy | No (absence category) | Auto-declines meetings |
| Personal mirror | "Busy" copy, details stripped | Busy | No | One-way, personal → work |

Muhlert is on Business Standard ([Issue #40](https://github.com/Y-O-W/dotfiles/issues/40)), which is the minimum edition for **Focus time**, including auto-decline and Chat "do not disturb" ([Google Help: Focus time](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/11190973?hl=en)), and for multiple appointment schedules with co-hosts ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/16287038?hl=en)). Free events are the key design choice. Booking pages and "Find a time" skip Free time, so flexible work stays movable. The trade-off is that a Free event protects nothing, and agencies commonly fail by marking personal blocks as Free. The user's own research already reached the same rule: fixed and protected events are Busy, flexible events are Free, and **agent-created flexible events are always Free** ([vault note](file:///Users/yowfactor/Obsidian/Software/software_library/software_calendar.md)). Working location is visible to anyone with free/busy access ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/7638168?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop)), so it should be enabled for the Tuesday–Thursday office rhythm ([Muhlert Notes page](https://app.notion.com/p/39fed4002a8080739409fa5ced3a3e00)).

### The title grammar: `CLIENT SERVICE: activity`

The proposed grammar is two upper-case codes, a colon, then free text. Examples: `TLN SEA: Weekly performance call`, `TLN DSP: Monthly report prep`, `LEI DES: Banner round 2`, `MD ADM: Productive setup`, `OCC PRE: Pitch workshop`, `NT: Renew passkey`, `HOLD TLN SOC: Q1 planning`. The pattern is easy to type on a phone, simple for an agent to parse (`^(HOLD )?([A-Z0-9]{2,5}) ([A-Z]{2,4}): (.+)$`, with `NT:` as the untracked escape), and reads acceptably when a client sees the invite. Client codes should mirror Productive company or budget names and live in one maintained list that agents can read, such as a Productive custom field or a Notion table. Service codes mirror Muhlert's services: SEA (Paid Search), SOC (Paid Social), PRG (Programmatic), SEO, ANA (Analytics), DES (Ad Creative/Design), DSP (Digital Strategy & Planning, the project-management service that sits on every budget) ([Productive Setup page](https://app.notion.com/p/3abed4002a8080dd8865e3daff46ab46)). Three reserved client codes complete the scheme: `MD` for internal Muhlert time (the internal "Agency" budget), `PRE` as a service code for presales time booked on deals, and `NT` for untracked items. Writing "reporting" under a specific service code once, in the code list, settles Nina's "Where do I book reporting?" question for everyone. Recurring meetings should keep **stable titles**, because Productive's AI matches recurring events against past entries. For agent-created events, put a `[claude-plan]` tag in the description, as epic #40 already proposes, so humans can see what an agent wrote and agents can find their own drafts.

### Colours are a personal legend, not a team signal

Because Google colours are per-user and invisible to attendees, a colour legend helps each person read their own week and their Time Insights breakdown. It cannot coordinate the team. The recommended approach is one shared legend that encodes **one dimension only**: billability. Client billable, client non-billable or PM (DSP), presales, internal (MD), personal or private, and untracked (NT) each get one hue, published as hex values so everyone sets the same custom colours. Event types should not get colours, because Show as and Focus time already convey them visually. Colour labels need a work account and can be disabled by an admin ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/12377581)), so check the Muhlert admin setting before publishing the legend.

### Focus and meeting norms fit a Tuesday–Thursday office week

Muhlert's office days are Tuesday to Thursday ([Muhlert Notes page](https://app.notion.com/p/39fed4002a8080739409fa5ced3a3e00)), which already concentrates collaboration in the middle of the week. The proposal below makes that concentration explicit. **Internal meetings fall inside a core window on office days** (for example 10:00–16:00, Tuesday to Thursday), a scaled-down version of Dropbox's four-hour Core Collaboration Hours ([Dropbox](https://experience.dropbox.com/virtualfirst/model)). **Monday and Friday mornings are internal-meeting-free** and appear as a recurring all-team event, which is Asana's device for making a no-meeting day visible ([Asana](https://asana.com/inside-asana/workstyle-no-meeting-wednesdays)). Client meetings are exempt up front, as GitLab's Field team does for its Focus Fridays ([GitLab](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/sales/sales-meetings/)). This keeps the team well short of the "too many meeting-free days" failure mode, and the fragmentation and residue evidence supports the clustering itself. Meetings default to 25 or 50 minutes via Google's speedy-meetings setting, which Google's own hybrid-work guidance recommends ([Google Workspace Learning Center](https://support.google.com/a/users/answer/10985697?hl=en)). Every internal invite links an agenda ([GitLab](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/live-doc-meetings)), and anyone may decline an invite without an agenda, which Dropbox and Shopify both made explicit. Everyone runs at least one Focus time block per day on non-office days. The 25/50 default and the no-meeting mornings are untested conventions, so the team should treat them as experiments to review, not settled rules.

### Planning rituals keep work planning inside working hours

For the team, three rituals are enough. The **weekly plan** takes 20–30 minutes on Monday morning or Friday afternoon, in working time. Each person reviews last week's planned-versus-actual time, names three outcomes, and turns each into a dated block sized from Productive actuals plus a buffer. This applies implementation intentions and the planning-fallacy correction. The **daily shutdown** takes 10–15 minutes at the end of the workday. Each person converts the day's events into Productive entries (or presses the AI button), marks blocks done or carried over, writes a one-line ready-to-resume note for any unfinished block, and checks tomorrow for conflicts. The **quarterly calendar retro** takes 15 minutes. The team purges recurring meetings, each person reviews their own Time Insights meeting load, and the team adjusts the norms. Shopify's purge of recurring meetings plus reinforcement tooling cut per-person meeting time by 14% ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/07/14/shopify-cfo-meeting-cost-calculator/)). Even Dropbox reached only 81% partial adoption after a year ([Dropbox](https://blog.dropbox.com/topics/company/what-weve-learned-our-first-year-as-a-virtual-first-company)), so the team should expect drift and plan for reinforcement.

For the user personally, the Sunday and evening sessions on the Notion page are a sound structure for life planning. Their **work** part, however, conflicts with the detachment evidence and, for employees, with the expectation that working-time recording captures all work time. The recommendation is to keep Sunday for personal and life planning and to move the work part of the week to the Monday or Friday team ritual. The evening session should become the end-of-workday shutdown, plus a short personal check later in the evening that only reads the work calendar and never edits it.

### Time-tracking cadence: same-day conversion, weekly approval

The evidence on accuracy favours same-day logging. Recall estimates of "usual" hours run about **5–10% above time diaries** ([BLS 2011](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2011/06/art3full.pdf)), while recall of specific recent hours is close to diary accuracy ([BLS 2014](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2014/article/is-the-workweek-really-overestimated.htm)), and billing guidance agrees that contemporaneous recording is most accurate ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/publications/law_practice_magazine/2019/mj2019/mj19keeler/)). The cadence is: convert calendar events in Productive's My Time view during the daily shutdown, set Productive reminders to daily with a tolerant untracked-time threshold ([Productive Help](https://help.productive.io/en/articles/4031347-setting-up-time-tracking-reminders)), and submit and approve weekly, since only approved time counts toward budget usage. Draft entries are private until finished, and **submitting a timesheet permanently deletes unfinished drafts** ([Productive Help](https://help.productive.io/en/articles/10627900-submitting-time-entries-for-approval-employee-guide)), so the weekly submission must come after the drafts are completed. Productive's Automatic Time Tracking (Ultimate plan) converts **bookings** into entries at midnight ([Productive Help](https://help.productive.io/en/articles/3158736-automatic-time-tracking)). That records the plan, not the work, so leave it off unless people review the entries each day. Report utilisation at team level and discuss individual numbers one-to-one. Billable-utilisation benchmarks across professional services sit around **66% (2025) against a 75% "high performer" mark** ([Deltek/SPI](https://www.deltek.com/resources/articles/professional-services-billable-utilization-historic-low/)), and targets should vary by role.

## German law turns calendar time tracking into working-time recording

The CJEU's 2019 *CCOO v Deutsche Bank* ruling requires member states to oblige employers to run an **"objective, reliable and accessible" system that records each worker's daily working time** ([CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2019-05/cp190061en.pdf)). In Germany, the BAG ruled on 13 September 2022 (1 ABR 22/21) that this duty already applies under § 3(2) no. 1 ArbSchG. Employers must record **start, end and duration**, including overtime. They may delegate the recording to employees, and the form is open for now. The duty probably excludes senior executives (leitende Angestellte), and § 25 ArbSchG provides no direct fine ([CMS](https://cms.law/de/deu/legal-updates/Arbeitszeiterfassung-fuer-fast-alle!-Entscheidungsgruende-des-BAG-veroeffentlicht); [Freshfields 2022](https://freshfields.com/de/unser-denken/briefings/2022/12/bag-zur-arbeitszeiterfassung--sofortige-pflicht-mit-vielen-fragen)). A BMAS draft amendment to the ArbZG would require **electronic, same-day** recording, with transition periods and possible relief for small firms. As of the latest sources it is **not law**, and its timetable keeps slipping ([Haufe](https://www.haufe.de/id/beitrag/gesetzesradar-12-arbeitszeitreform-HI17210257.html); [Freshfields 2026](https://www.freshfields.com/de/unser-denken/briefings/2026/06/ruckschritt-bei-der-arbeitszeitreform)).

This has three practical consequences for the playbook. The first follows from the law; the second and third are inferences that a German employment lawyer should confirm. First, **Productive project hours are not automatically a working-time record**. Hours per service say nothing about when the workday started and ended, and they omit unbooked time. Entries converted from calendar events carry real start and end times, which helps. Muhlert still needs either full-day coverage of time ranges or a separate attendance record, and the 23.11.26 note that Factorial might handle time tracking suggests that route is already under consideration ([Muhlert Notes page](https://app.notion.com/p/39fed4002a8080739409fa5ced3a3e00)). Doing this the same day now also prepares for the draft law. Second, the recording duty attaches to employees, so freelancers on client teams sit outside it. The playbook should still apply to them for billing, but without the attendance element. Third, **if Muhlert has a works council**, it co-determines the design of the recording system (§ 87(1) no. 7 BetrVG) and any technical system able to monitor behaviour or performance (§ 87(1) no. 6). The BAG has applied no. 6 even to an Excel sheet ([Dr. Datenschutz](https://www.dr-datenschutz.de/mitbestimmungsrecht-des-betriebsrats-auch-bei-zeiterfassung-mit-excel/)). Calendar sync, reminders, approval workflows and AI conversion would then need a works agreement before rollout. The notes do not say whether a works council exists.

GDPR shapes the same choices. No calendar-specific guidance from a data-protection authority was found, so this paragraph is an inference from general principles. Calendar events and time entries are personal data. The playbook should state each purpose (working-time compliance, billing, estimating, workload protection), rule out reuse for covert performance ranking, set retention periods, and list the processors involved (Google, Productive, and the AI features in both, plus Claude when it is connected). The monitoring research gives the business case for this transparency. Electronic monitoring meta-analyses find **no performance gain from monitoring as such** (r = −.01) ([Siegel et al. 2022](https://scidok.sulb.uni-saarland.de/bitstream/20.500.11880/34042/1/1-s2.0-S2451958822000616-main.pdf)). Workers find it stressful whatever its form, but "organizations that monitor more transparently and less invasively can expect more positive attitudes" ([Ravid et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/07/NFW-Ravid-et-al.pdf)). Self-entered project time with a stated purpose sits at the low-invasiveness end. App or screen capture tools such as Timing sit at the other end and should stay out of the team setup. Two more privacy details: personal-to-work mirrors must strip titles, because health appointments are sensitive, and in the opposite direction, Muhlert's management goal that an AI agent "can reach every surface" ([Muhlert Notes page](https://app.notion.com/p/39fed4002a8080739409fa5ced3a3e00)) needs a restricted AI user and write approvals, as the Productive setup notes already propose.

## Keep the playbook where people and agents can both reach it

The playbook only stays consistent if it lives in one place that the team reads and agents can reach. Muhlert works in Google Workspace, not Notion, so the team layer belongs in Google Docs. The setup splits into four layers, each stored next to the system it describes. This is a design recommendation, not a research finding.

| Layer | Contents | Where it lives | How agents reach it | Who changes it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Team playbook | Event types, Busy/Free rules, title grammar, meeting norms, rituals, tracking cadence | One Google Doc in a Muhlert shared drive | Google Docs MCP: read, suggest edits, comment. Google Drive connector: check revisions | A named owner (for example Nina) accepts or rejects; agents only suggest |
| 2. Codes | Client codes, service codes, reserved codes (`MD`, `PRE`, `NT`, `HOLD`) | Productive: a "Calendar code" custom field on each company; service codes on services | Productive MCP, read live during planning | Whoever creates the client or service in Productive |
| 3. Procedures | Weekly plan, daily shutdown, converting events to time entries | Claude skills: a team skill, plus the user's personal skill in dotfiles | Loaded when a session starts | Playbook owner (team), user (personal) |
| 4. Personal conventions | The user's four calendars, YOWfactor rules, the Busy mirror | Vault note and dotfiles, as epic #40 plans | The user's own skills | The user |

Three design rules hold the layers together. First, **codes live in Productive, not in the document**. Productive already owns clients, budgets and services, and a renamed service is exactly what breaks the AI's recurring-event matching, so a code stored on the company record cannot drift from a separate list. Whether Productive supports custom fields on companies should be checked before rollout; if it does not, the codes go in a table in the playbook. Second, **skills reference the playbook instead of copying it**: a skill says "read the playbook and the codes, then plan", so a rule changes in one place. Third, **the document must have an owner account that won't go away**: a shared drive, not a personal My Drive, for the same reason team calendars need a durable owner.

Two connectors shape how agents update the document. The **Google Drive connector** (connected today) can read a Doc's content and comments, list its revisions and create new files, but it cannot edit an existing Doc. Google's official **Docs MCP server** closes that gap. Its `read_doc` tool returns the document's full structure, and `update_doc` accepts a Docs API `batchUpdate` request ([Google Docs MCP reference](https://developers.google.com/workspace/docs/api/reference/mcp?hl=en)). That request can write edits **as suggestions** instead of direct changes, and can **insert comments** on a text range, optionally assigned to a person, and reply to or resolve them. Accepting a suggestion requires edit access to the document; rejecting one requires edit access or authorship ([Docs API: comments and suggestions](https://developers.google.com/workspace/docs/api/how-tos/suggestions?hl=en)). This maps directly onto "agents propose, people decide":

1. **Read.** At the start of a weekly plan or shutdown, the skill reads the playbook and notes its latest revision. If the revision changed since the last run, the agent summarises what changed before planning.
2. **Spot drift.** The agent compares the calendar with the rules: unparseable titles, a new Productive client without a code, a recurring meeting whose title changed, holds older than 48 hours.
3. **Suggest.** After the user approves, the agent writes the proposed change into the playbook as a suggested edit. It adds a comment, assigned to the playbook owner, that cites what it saw on the calendar. A Slack message to the owner is optional, only as a notification.
4. **Decide.** The owner accepts or rejects the suggestion in Google Docs as usual and updates the changelog line at the top with the date and version number. Code changes go straight into Productive, where an agent can write them through the Productive MCP after approval.

**Enforce the boundary with permissions, not instructions.** Give the account the agent uses **comment-only access** to the playbook. It can then suggest and comment but can neither edit directly nor accept its own suggestions, so Google, not the agent's instructions, enforces the review step. Google does not say whether API suggestions work with comment-only access, so test this before relying on it.

Four caveats apply. First, the Docs MCP server is a **Developer Preview**, available only through Google's Workspace Developer Preview Program. Its documentation is silent on authentication, admin requirements and limits, so expect enrolment, changes and no service guarantee. Until it is generally available, keep a fallback: the agent posts its proposal in Slack, and the owner edits by hand. Second, it is not connected in this setup yet; the capabilities above come from Google's documentation and have not been tested. Third, suggest mode does not cover tab or header/footer changes or document formatting, which is acceptable for a rules document. Fourth, a batch update can partly fail, so the agent must check the save status in the response before reporting a suggestion or comment as made.

Inside the Doc, keep prose for people plus one clearly marked block of structured rules (the title pattern, the event-type and Busy/Free table, the reserved codes), so agents can parse it reliably. Link to Productive for the codes rather than repeating them. Two access details follow from Q3. If the Drive connector or the Docs MCP server is signed in to the YOWfactor account, the playbook Doc or its folder must be shared with that account, which again depends on Muhlert's external sharing settings, this time for Drive. Agents should also treat the Doc's text, and especially its comments, as data, not instructions, under the same prompt-injection rule as calendar invites.

## Roll it out as a measured six-week trial

Conventions spread through visible artefacts and reinforcement, not through a one-off announcement. Shopify paired its purge with a Slack bot that flagged meetings booked on the no-meeting day ([Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/9403144/shopify-meetings-cancelled-productivity)), and Dropbox let teams shift their collaboration window ([Dropbox](https://experience.dropbox.com/virtualfirst/model)). The rollout should follow the order of Muhlert's Productive priorities, where automated time tracking comes before task management because task management "requires changing behavior from the whole team" ([Productive Setup page](https://app.notion.com/p/3abed4002a8080dd8865e3daff46ab46)). Week 0: publish the one-page playbook and the client and service code list, check the works-council and attendance-record position, and connect every calendar to Productive. Weeks 1–2: adopt the title grammar and the busy/free rules only. Measure the share of events with a parseable title and the share of time converted the same day. Weeks 3–4: add the meeting window, the no-meeting mornings and the speedy defaults, and run the recurring-meeting purge. Weeks 5–6: add AI conversion and agent-drafted plans under propose-then-apply rules. Then hold a retro that compares meeting load in Time Insights and same-day logging rates with the baseline. Agency-specific failure modes to watch are client meetings creeping into focus mornings, holds that are never released, personal blocks set to Free, and title drift on recurring meetings, which quietly breaks Productive's AI matching.

## Your calendar today: audit and step-by-step optimisation

This chapter applies the report to the user's own calendars. It is based on a read-only pass over all calendars visible to the YOWfactor connector for 10 November to 7 December 2026, read on 24.11.26. Nothing was changed. Personal and medical details are deliberately left out.

### What the calendars look like

| Calendar | Events (4 weeks) | How it is used | Main issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Muhlert Digital - Work | 154 | Past weeks fully blocked, about 8 h per day from 09:00; 25 meetings; prep and follow-up blocks around meetings; daily planning at 09:00 | Access role `reader`; titles not machine-readable; Busy/Free inconsistent; working location recorded twice; future weeks barely planned |
| yowfactor_business (YOWfactor primary) | ~30 | Working location (Office Tue–Thu, Home Mon/Fri, set up in 2023); monthly finance deadlines as all-day Free events | Reminders stored as events; working location duplicated across accounts |

### What already works

- **The Muhlert calendar is a real time diary.** Past weeks are covered almost completely from the start to the end of the day. This is the same-day logging the evidence favours, and it already gives Productive start and end times, which the working-time recording duty needs.
- **Titles follow a hierarchy.** Almost every event uses "Area - Topic - Activity" (for example "Muhlert - Productive - Testing" or "Health - Strength - Barbell"). Switching to the playbook grammar is a change of format, not of habit.
- **Meetings get preparation and follow-up blocks.** This is a good fit for the ready-to-resume note: the follow-up block is the natural place to write it.
- **Daily planning is already a fixed ritual** at 09:00 for 30 minutes, and working location is in use.

### What conflicts with the playbook

1. **No write access.** The connector has `reader` access to the Muhlert calendar, so an agent cannot create blocks there yet (see Q3).
2. **Titles cannot be parsed reliably.** Internal work starts with "Muhlert -" instead of a code, client work appears in several formats for the same client (for example "Klang/Shopware - Briefing", "Klang - Meeting", "Klang x MD"), and the Productive tests in the Gmail calendar used long "Client - Budget - Service" names.
3. **Busy/Free is inconsistent.** "Muhlert - Daily Planning" is Busy on 7 days and Free on 13. Comparable work blocks are sometimes Busy and sometimes Free.
4. **Working location is recorded twice.** The Muhlert calendar has both working-location status events ("Office", "Home") and all-day events "Muhlert - Yves - Office/Remote". The YOWfactor primary calendar has its own working-location pattern too.
5. **The weekly plan does not reach the calendar yet.** Past weeks are full, but the coming weeks hold only 0.5–2 hours of blocks per day. Planning happens day by day; only one weekly planning block appears in four weeks.
6. **Meetings cluster on Monday late morning.** All Hands (10:30), the weekly 1:1 and the Klang internal meeting all sit on Monday. On 30 November and 7 December the 1:1 and the Klang internal meeting overlap at 11:00. Two meetings started at 17:30. No Focus time events exist.
7. **The routine calendar blocks the whole day.** About 16 Busy events per day would block every hour if they were mirrored, and its generic work blocks compete with the real plan on the Muhlert calendar.
8. **Reminders and lists live in events.** Household chores, tax and payroll deadlines, rent, backups and payments are all-day events; shopping and to-do lists live in event descriptions.
9. **Sensitive notes sit in event descriptions.** Medical details in the Health calendar would be read by any agent with access and would leak through any mirror that copies descriptions.
10. **Options are marked Busy, and test data remains.** Recurring markets and meetups block time as if they were commitments, and fake-client test events remain in the Gmail calendar.

The audit also affects the team playbook. Its proposal of internal-meeting-free Monday mornings collides with the existing Monday All Hands at 10:30. Either All Hands moves, or the protected slots become Monday before 10:30 plus Friday morning.

### Step-by-step optimisation

**Phase 1: Access and clean-up (week 1)**

1. Ask the Muhlert admin for the "outsiders can change calendars" level. Then share the Muhlert calendar with the YOWfactor account as "Make changes to events" and test by creating and deleting one block.
2. Fix the overlapping 1:1 and Klang internal meeting on 30 November and 7 December.
3. Delete the all-day "Muhlert - Yves - Office/Remote" events and keep only the working-location status in the Muhlert account (Office Tue–Thu, Home Mon/Fri).
4. Once Productive testing is finished, delete the fake-client test events from the Gmail calendar.

**Phase 2: Fewer calendars, no reminders in events (weeks 1–2)**

5. Consolidate to six own calendars: Muhlert Work, YOWfactor business, Personal, Health, Routine/Plan and Events. Merge yowfactor_event and yowfactor_business_event into Events, with Free as the default. Retire yowfactor_personal_event: show birthdays from Google Contacts and move the payment reminder to Tasks.
6. Move every reminder into Google Tasks as a recurring task: household chores, tax and payroll deadlines, bank and invoice checks, rent, backups and payments.
7. Move the shopping and to-do lists out of event descriptions into the Google Docs that are already attached to those events, and delete the two list events.
8. Move medical notes out of Health event descriptions into a private document. Events keep only title, time and location.

**Phase 3: Busy/Free and the routine calendar (week 2)**

9. Set all Routine events to Free and trim the template to anchors that matter (sleep, sport, commute, reading). Delete the generic "Deep Work", "Business Work" and "Work - Meeting" blocks: real work blocks belong on the Muhlert or YOWfactor calendar.
10. Set recurring options in Events (markets, run club, meetups) to Free; switch an option to Busy only once you commit to it.
11. On the Muhlert calendar, apply the playbook rule consistently: meetings and protected blocks Busy, flexible work Free. Make Daily Planning a recurring Busy event, and set up deep-work blocks as Focus time.

**Phase 4: Titles (weeks 2–3)**

12. Use `CLIENT SERVICE: activity` for every event you create on the Muhlert calendar, with `MD` replacing the "Muhlert -" prefix for internal work. Examples: "Muhlert - Productive - Testing" becomes `MD ADM: Productive testing`; "Klang/Shopware - Briefing - Meeting Preparation" becomes `<KLANG CODE> DSP: Shopware briefing prep`, using the client code from Productive. Meetings organised by others keep their titles, which you cannot change; Productive's AI matches recurring meetings anyway.
13. Keep the existing "Area - Topic - Activity" style on personal calendars. Those events are never time-tracked, and mirrored copies appear on the Muhlert calendar only as "Busy".

**Phase 5: Planning rhythm (from week 3)**

14. Move the weekly plan into working time (Friday afternoon or Monday at 09:00) and have it produce next week's blocks, sized from Productive actuals plus a buffer. A planned week, not just a planned day, is the goal.
15. Keep Daily Planning at 09:00 and add a recurring end-of-day shutdown at about 16:30 on the Muhlert calendar: convert the day's events in Productive, mark blocks done or carried over, and write resume notes.
16. Keep Muhlert work out of the evening routine. YOWfactor administration in the evening is your own business's time, but it should not become a second Muhlert planning session (see the detachment evidence).

**Phase 6: Mirror and agent (once write access exists)**

17. Set up the one-way Busy mirror: Personal and Health events, plus committed Events, become titleless "Busy" blocks on the Muhlert calendar. Routine (Free) and Tasks are not mirrored.
18. Configure the planning agent: it reads Muhlert, YOWfactor, Personal, Routine and Events; it reads Health only as free/busy; it writes only guest-free blocks on the Muhlert calendar and events on the YOWfactor calendars, after approval.
19. Test whether the agent can create Focus time on the Muhlert calendar. If not, keep a recurring Focus time block, for example on Monday and Friday afternoons, created in the Muhlert account itself.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing more than it changes the tools. The calendar's value for Muhlert lies less in personal productivity, where effects are modest and partly reflect personality, than in **data quality and felt control**: a title that names client and service turns the calendar into a same-day time diary that a human or an agent can convert into billable, approvable, legally useful records. That makes the naming convention a piece of infrastructure, not a style preference. It belongs next to the Productive service catalogue and should be versioned with it, since a renamed service breaks the AI's recurring-event matching just as a renamed column breaks a report.

Two open risks deserve the most attention. The first is legal: once calendar-derived entries are used as attendance evidence or connected to AI conversion, a works council (if one exists) and GDPR purpose limitation apply, so those decisions should be made before rollout, not after. The second is evidential: nobody has tested shared focus norms or calendar transparency in teams. Muhlert's six-week trial will produce better evidence for its own context than the literature can, provided the team measures parseable titles, same-day logging and meeting load before and after.
